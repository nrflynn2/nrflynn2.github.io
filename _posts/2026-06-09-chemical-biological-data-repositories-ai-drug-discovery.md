Every machine-learning model in drug discovery is a function of the data it was trained on. The surge of AI in this field rides on the sheer volume of data now being generated — but availability cuts both ways. Scarce high-quality labels, class imbalance, unreported negative results, and incomplete biological annotations all impose hard ceilings on what a model can learn. The space of drug-like molecules is enormous, the variety of biological targets is vast, and the properties we care about are high-dimensional. It is not unusual to commit to a research direction only to find a dearth of usable data waiting for you.

This is a catalog of the contemporary, publicly accessible repositories worth tracking as your own projects take shape. Most hold existing compounds; a few enumerate billions of *virtual molecules* that do not yet exist but could be synthesized. It is not meant to be an exhaustive reference of benchmark datasets — those are in flux, and task-specific sets are best found in the primary literature — but it is a map of the landscape, organized the way I actually reach for these resources: by what you are trying to model.

> Adapted from Appendix B of my book, [Build AI Drug Discovery Pipelines](/book/) — one of the freely readable appendices that most readers never open. I keep this catalog here, rather than freezing it in print, because it is the chapter most likely to go stale. **Last updated: 2026-06-09.**

## How to read this catalog

Each section opens with a quick-reference table — what the resource is, its rough scale, and its license or access terms — followed by notes on the handful of resources you will reach for most often. The "additional resources" rows round out each category without a full write-up. Scales and counts are 2025 snapshots and will drift; that is exactly why this page is maintained rather than printed once. Where a resource is most useful in the book, I point to the chapter.

## Bioactivity and binding affinity

This is where most supervised models in early discovery get their labels: measured activities and affinities between molecules and targets.

| Resource | What it is | Scale (2025) | License / access |
|---|---|---|---|
| [ChEMBL](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) | Manually curated bioactivity from the medicinal-chemistry literature | ~20M activities · 2.5M compounds · 15.6K targets (v35) | Open (CC BY-SA); REST API, bulk |
| [ChEBI](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chebi/) | Dictionary of small-molecule entities of biological interest | ~60K entities | Open; co-maintained with ChEMBL |
| [BindingDB](https://www.bindingdb.org/) | Measured binding affinities (Ki, Kd, IC50, EC50, thermodynamics), incl. patents | 3.29M datapoints · 1.35M molecules · 9,454 targets | Open; web, REST API, bulk, monthly |
| [Guide to Pharmacology (GtoPdb)](https://www.guidetopharmacology.org/) | Expert-curated targets, ligands, quantitative interactions | ~3K targets · 12K ligands · 23K interactions | Open; web, REST API, bulk |
| [PubChem BioAssay](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioassay/) | NIH/academic/industry high-throughput screening data | Hundreds of millions of datapoints | Public |
| [BindingMOAD](http://www.bindingmoad.org/) | Curated protein–ligand complexes with binding data | ~41K complexes (static since 2020) | Open |
| [PDBbind](http://www.pdbbind.org.cn/) | PDB subset with measured affinities (general / refined / core) | Long-standing docking benchmark | Open |

**ChEMBL and ChEBI.** ChEMBL is the workhorse open bioactivity warehouse — high-quality, manually curated, drawn primarily from medicinal-chemistry papers, and spanning 40+ years of published research. Compound structures are stored as standard SMILES and InChI and targets are linked to UniProt, so the data drops straight into cheminformatics tooling like RDKit. ChEBI, its co-maintained sibling, is a dictionary of small-molecule entities of biological interest (hence the name).

> **FAIR data principles.** ChEMBL is built around FAIR — *findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable* — and that is what makes it so useful for ML. *Findable:* persistent identifiers and rich metadata let you pull, say, every measurement for a kinase family programmatically. *Accessible:* a REST API replaces scraping PDFs. *Interoperable:* standard SMILES/InChI and UniProt links let you merge across databases without a brittle preprocessing layer. *Reusable:* provenance and assay metadata let you filter by experimental condition or model assay type as an explicit feature. The payoff is reproducibility — test sets built with awareness of provenance, and models benchmarked on common, well-understood data rather than private, inconsistently processed collections.

**BindingDB** curates measured affinities from both the literature and issued US patents — a patent-derived slice ChEMBL does not systematically cover. It overlaps with ChEMBL (roughly 55% of ChEMBL's data is also here), but the patent-derived and mass-spectrometry-measured affinities buy breadth when a model needs more than literature-curated activities.

**The Guide to Pharmacology** is the reference for expert-curated target pharmacology, jointly maintained by IUPHAR and the British Pharmacological Society. The World Health Organization draws on it for standardized drug nomenclature; it prioritizes carefully reviewed target-family definitions and quantitative interaction data over raw scale.

## Chemical structure and property databases

When you need chemical identity, physicochemical properties, or drug-level annotation rather than assay readouts.

| Resource | What it is | Scale (2025) | License / access |
|---|---|---|---|
| [PubChem](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) | NIH/NLM federated hub of compounds, substances, bioactivities | ~119M compounds · 302M substances · 304M bioactivities · 903 sources | Public; daily updates |
| [DrugBank](https://go.drugbank.com/) | Structural + pharmacological + target data on drugs | ~15K drugs (2.7K FDA small molecules, 1.5K biologics, 6.7K experimental) | Free tier; **commercial license** for full DB |
| [DrugCentral](https://drugcentral.org/) | Openly licensed companion: approved-drug regulatory, pharmacology, indications | ~4,900 approved drugs | Open (CC BY-SA) |
| [ChemSpider](http://www.chemspider.com/) | RSC-aggregated chemical structures | ~115M structures · 276 sources | Free |
| [HMDB](https://hmdb.ca/) | Human metabolome (metabolites + linked proteins) | ~221K metabolites · 8.6K proteins | Open |
| [NIST Chemistry WebBook](https://webbook.nist.gov/) | Thermochemical, reaction, and spectroscopic data | — | Open |
| [SureChEMBL](https://www.surechembl.org/) | Chemical structures mined from worldwide patents | ~28M structures, daily updates | Open |

**PubChem** is the federally sponsored giant, maintained by the National Library of Medicine. Beyond chemical structures it links out to genes, proteins, pathways, and literature, carries a dedicated COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2 subset, and — since 2019 — absorbed **ToxNet**, bringing hazardous-substance, genetic-toxicology, and carcinogenesis data under one roof.

**DrugBank and DrugCentral.** DrugBank is the comprehensive drug-centric resource, but the full database requires a commercial license — something to factor in early if you intend to publish or commercialize a pipeline. DrugCentral, maintained by the University of New Mexico, is the redistribution-friendly companion: openly licensed, strong on marketed drugs, more limited on experimental ones. The two are commonly paired when licensing matters.

## Structural biology

Cryo-EM, NMR, and X-ray crystallography give the atomic-level picture of how a drug meets its target — the gold standard for structure-based design.

| Resource | What it is | Scale (2025) | License / access |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Protein Data Bank (PDB)](https://www.rcsb.org/) | Experimentally resolved 3D structures of proteins, DNA, RNA, and complexes | 230,000+ structures | Open |
| [AlphaFold DB](https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) | AlphaFold2-predicted protein structures with pLDDT/PAE confidence | ~214M structures (v4), covers UniProt | Open (DeepMind / EMBL-EBI) |
| [EMDB](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/emdb/) | Cryo-EM and cryo-ET maps | 48,000+ maps | Open |
| [BMRB](https://bmrb.io/) | NMR data for proteins, nucleic acids, metabolites | — | Open |
| [UniProt](https://www.uniprot.org/) | Reference protein sequence and functional annotation | — | Open |
| [InterPro / Pfam / CATH / SCOP](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) | Protein domain, family, and structural classification | InterPro now subsumes Pfam | Open |
| [OPM](https://opm.phar.umich.edu/) | Orientation of membrane proteins in lipid bilayers | — | Open |
| [ASD](http://mdl.shsmu.edu.cn/ASD/) | Allosteric proteins, sites, and modulators | — | Open |
| [CSD](https://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/) · [COD](http://www.crystallography.net/cod/) | Small-molecule crystallographic databases (commercial / open) | — | CSD commercial; COD open |

**The Protein Data Bank** is the canonical repository of experimentally resolved macromolecular structures, including proteins with bound small-molecule ligands — and it was one of the data sources used to train AlphaFold.

**AlphaFold DB** changed the scale at which structure-informed discovery can operate, from ~230K experimental structures to two orders of magnitude more, spanning nearly every sequenced organism. Each prediction ships with per-residue pLDDT confidence and predicted aligned error (PAE) maps, which you should inspect before committing to any structure-based modeling — the kind of judgment call I work through in [druggability assessment with AlphaFold 3]({{ '/blog/2026/druggability-assessment-alphafold-3/' | relative_url }}). The book leans on AlphaFold DB throughout Chapter 12 and Appendix D as a complement to experimental structures.

> **Experimental vs. predicted structures.** Keep the distinction sharp. Experimental structures (X-ray, cryo-EM, NMR) are direct physical evidence — typically 1–3 Å for high-quality models — and capture specific conformational states along with water molecules, metal ions, and binding-site flexibility. AlphaFold DB structures are *predictions* without per-protein experimental validation; AlphaFold2 is remarkably accurate for well-conserved folds but less reliable elsewhere. For structure-based drug design, prioritize experimental structures when available, treat predictions as valuable stand-ins where data is missing, watch the pLDDT scores on novel target classes and predicted pockets, and validate computationally derived structures orthogonally before pouring resources into a campaign.

## Ultra-large virtual libraries

Make-on-demand chemistry has pushed screenable space into the tens of billions — molecules that do not sit on a shelf but can realistically be synthesized and assayed.

| Resource | What it is | Scale (2025) | License / access |
|---|---|---|---|
| [ZINC22](https://cartblanche22.docking.org/) | Tangible, 3D-ready molecules for large-scale virtual screening | ~54.9B molecules | Free; tranche download, API, web |
| [Enamine REAL Space](https://enamine.net/compound-collections/real-compounds) | Largest enumerated make-on-demand library (~80% synthesis success) | ~81.8B (REAL Database subset ~6.75B) | Free for academic; purchasable |
| [GDB-17](https://gdb.unibe.ch/downloads/) | Exhaustive enumeration of molecules up to 17 heavy atoms | ~166.4B molecules | Open |

**ZINC22** is the current staple for ultra-large 3D virtual screening — the successor to ZINC15/20. Every molecule is in stock, available on synthesis-on-demand terms, or sourced from curated vendor catalogs, so docking hits can actually be purchased and tested. It incorporates make-on-demand catalogs such as Enamine REAL and powers most contemporary ultra-large docking campaigns, including the ones the book builds in Chapters 2, 7, 9, and 10.

**Enamine REAL Space** is the make-on-demand library itself, enumerated from a small set of validated reactions and a curated building-block inventory, with roughly 80% synthesis success on actual orders and 2–6 week delivery. It is the compound pool behind landmark ultra-large docking studies (Lyu et al. 2019; Sadybekov et al. 2022, *Nature*), and it is freely downloadable for academic use — directly or through ZINC22.

## ML benchmark suites

How you evaluate matters as much as what you train. This category has been the site of the field's most important self-correction: the move away from leaky, optimistic benchmarks toward leakage-aware ones.

| Benchmark | Focus | Size / scope | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [MoleculeNet](https://moleculenet.org/) | Molecular property prediction (QM, physchem, biophysics, physiology) | 17 datasets · 700K+ compounds | Heavily cited; **documented leakage** (HIV, BACE) |
| [Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC)](https://tdcommons.ai/) | Single/multi-instance prediction + generation across the pipeline | 66+ datasets · 22 tasks; TDC-2 multimodal | MIT; `pip install PyTDC` |
| [Polaris Hub](https://polarishub.io/) | Industrial-strength benchmarking with realistic splits | Industry-donated datasets | Built to fix MoleculeNet/TDC leakage; `polaris-lib` |
| [PoseBusters](https://github.com/maabuu/posebusters) | Physical validity of docking / co-folding poses | 308 complexes (post-2021) | MIT; standard for DiffDock, AF3, RFAA |
| [PLINDER](https://www.plinder.sh/) | Annotated protein–ligand interactions with similarity-aware splits | 449,383 complexes | MIT; the modern answer to docking leakage |
| [CrossDocked2020](https://github.com/gnina/models) | Cross-docked poses for structure-based generative training | ~22.5M poses · 18,450 complexes | Free; standard for Pocket2Mol, TargetDiff, DiffSBDD |
| [OGB](https://ogb.stanford.edu/) | Graph-ML benchmark (ogbg-molhiv, ogbg-molpcba) | — | Standard GNN harness |
| [GuacaMol](https://github.com/BenevolentAI/guacamol) · [MOSES](https://github.com/molecularsets/moses) | Generative-model benchmarks (distribution + goal-directed) | — | Chapter 10 |
| [LIT-PCBA](https://drugdesign.unistra.fr/LIT-PCBA/) | Virtual-screening benchmark from PubChem HTS | 15 targets | Preferred modern alternative to DUD-E |
| [DUD-E](https://dude.docking.org/) | Targets with actives + property-matched decoys | 102 targets | **Use cautiously:** documented ligand bias |
| [CASP](https://predictioncenter.org/) · [CAMEO](https://www.cameo3d.org/) | Blind structure-prediction assessment (biennial / weekly) | — | Where AlphaFold2 was demonstrated |

**MoleculeNet and TDC** remain the most heavily cited suites, and many papers still report on a subset of their tasks. But be aware of documented train/test leakage — HIV and BACE are the classic cases, and quality issues have proven pervasive — where scaffold splits alone fail to keep near-duplicates off both sides. The Therapeutics Data Commons is broader and ML-ready (`pip install PyTDC`, MIT-licensed), with TDC-2 extending into single-cell, clinical-trial, and protein–peptide data; it has become the default suite for much of the book's modeling, though it carries similar caveats.

**Polaris, PoseBusters, and PLINDER** are the response. **Polaris Hub** — backed by Valence Labs/Recursion, the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, and several pharma labs — was built specifically to fix the accumulated problems: duplicates, split leakage, over-optimistic random splits, and under-grounded industrial data. **PoseBusters** scores whether predicted poses are *physically* sensible (stereochemistry, bond geometry, clashes) rather than RMSD alone, and is now a standard reporting requirement for DiffDock, AlphaFold 3, and RoseTTAFold All-Atom. **PLINDER** is the largest annotated protein–ligand benchmark and ships similarity-aware splits that control for pocket, ligand, and protein similarity at once — the cleanest current defense against the leakage that inflated a decade of docking results. The book uses these in Chapters 9, 11, 12, and Appendix D.

## Toxicity and safety

Safety endpoints are where label quality and biological context matter most — and where naive modeling fails most quietly.

| Resource | What it is | Scale (2025) | License / access |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Tox21 / ToxCast / CompTox Dashboard](https://comptox.epa.gov/) | High-throughput in-vitro toxicity + hazard/exposure data | Tox21 ~10K cpds/60 assays · ToxCast ~9K/1,800 assays · CompTox ~1.2M chemicals | CC0; REST API, bulk, quarterly |
| [SIDER](http://sideeffects.embl.de/) | Drug side effects extracted from product labels | ~1,400 drugs · 5,800 terms | **Last full update 2015** — pair with FAERS |
| [FAERS](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/surveillance/fda-adverse-event-reporting-system-faers) | FDA adverse-event reports | Quarterly data files | Open |

**The Tox21/ToxCast/CompTox stack** is the dominant source of high-throughput in-vitro toxicity data for ML — a joint EPA/NIH/FDA effort, all CC0-licensed and API-accessible. It is the gold-standard training resource for toxicity-endpoint models, including the cytochrome P450 case study the book builds in Chapter 5.

## Reaction and synthesis

For retrosynthesis, forward prediction, and synthesizability — increasingly central as generative models propose molecules that someone has to actually make.

| Resource | What it is | Scale (2025) | License / access |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Open Reaction Database (ORD)](https://open-reaction-database.org/) | Schema-standardized open reaction data | 2M+ reactions | Open (CC BY-SA); protobuf schema, Python API |
| [USPTO / Lowe](https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Chemical_reactions_from_US_patents_1976-Sep2016_/5104873) | Reactions text-mined from US patents (1976–2016) | ~1.8M reactions | CC0; static at 2016 |
| Pistachio · Reaxys · SciFinder | Commercial continuations / reference reaction databases | — | **Commercial**, subscription only |

**The Open Reaction Database** — a Google Research / Princeton (Doyle) / MIT (Coley) collaboration — is the primary FAIR alternative to proprietary reaction databases, with a structured schema capturing reagents, conditions, outcomes, and provenance. **The USPTO/Lowe set** is the foundation training data for essentially every open retrosynthesis and forward-prediction model (Molecular Transformer, Chemformer, and successors), though its 2016 cutoff is its main limitation; the commercial **Pistachio** database is the direct continuation many industrial labs license.

**Open Targets** is the systematic target-identification platform — an EMBL-EBI / Wellcome Sanger / 13-company pharma consortium effort that fuses genetics, genomics, transcriptomics, drug, animal-model, and literature evidence into a single target–disease framework. A few neighbors are worth calling out for how they feed specific methods: **DepMap**'s CRISPR loss-of-function screens are the raw material for [synthetic-lethality prediction]({{ '/blog/2026/synthetic-lethality-drug-discovery-ml/' | relative_url }}); **CMap/LINCS** perturbational signatures are central to the [drug-repurposing methods]({{ '/blog/2026/drug-target-novelty-repurposing/' | relative_url }}) the book covers in Chapter 7; **GTEx** underpins [tissue-specific expression and target-safety analysis]({{ '/blog/2026/tissue-specificity-drug-target-safety/' | relative_url }}); and **GEO**'s single-cell holdings connect to the [virtual-cell models]({{ '/blog/2026/virtual-cells-target-discovery/' | relative_url }}) reshaping target discovery. Note KEGG's licensing: free for academic use within rate limits, but commercial use has required a license since 2022.

## Notes on usage

A catalog tells you what exists. It does not tell you whether the data is fit to model. Our models inherit the limitations of their training data, and a handful of properties are worth checking before you build anything.

### Primary vs. secondary sources

*Primary* data is the original experimental data submitted by researchers. *Secondary* data is built on top of it with additional analysis and interpretation — cheaper to assemble, often more general, and sometimes able to approximate information that is infeasible to measure directly. But secondary data can also inject noise from sources of questionable authenticity, relevance, or bias. In practice most databases are a *mix*, and disentangling the two is rarely straightforward. The way to judge reliability is **data provenance**: the record of how data was generated, transmitted, and stored, from origin to database entry.

### Data exchange and error propagation

Providers of data are also consumers of it. Public databases cross-reference and re-ingest each other constantly — [UniChem](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/unichem/) alone maps identifiers and structures across 40 databases including ChEMBL, ZINC, PubChem, HMDB, ChEBI, and DrugBank. That connectivity is powerful and dangerous: a single error can propagate across every linked resource, and the same molecule or unit can be represented inconsistently from one source to the next. When you integrate across sources, a few preprocessing habits prevent most of the damage:

1. Standardize units of measurement (e.g., convert all IC50 values to nM).
2. Normalize molecular representations with canonical SMILES or InChI.
3. Implement rigorous duplicate detection and resolution.
4. Establish clear rules for handling conflicting datapoints across sources.
5. Document provenance so every datapoint's origin is traceable.
6. Apply quality filters based on experimental confidence or statistical validation.

It also pays to read a database's own curation docs. ChEMBL, for instance, extracts reference, assay, target, organism, and structure information from full-text articles, then runs automated and manual curation to standardize activity types and units, compute properties, and flag or correct duplicates and errors. And mind **recency**: ChEMBL releases roughly twice a year, PubChem updates daily, some specialized databases only annually. A resource that has not been updated in 18 months may be missing exactly the emerging-target compounds you need — and updates often quietly fix earlier structural or activity errors, so record the version and timestamp of every dataset you train on.

### Train/test splits

A random split assumes training and test data are identically distributed, which is almost never true in drug discovery: chemical series cluster, assays drift over time, and literature coverage is biased toward well-studied targets. A **scaffold split** (Bemis–Murcko separation) removes obvious near-duplicates and is a sensible minimum bar, but it still lets near-identical analogs land on both sides. **Time-based splits** — train before a cutoff date, test after — better simulate prospective deployment and frequently reveal that headline numbers are badly inflated. **Similarity-aware splits**, which enforce separation by pocket, ligand, and protein similarity at once, are best practice for docking and structure-based ML; PLINDER implements them and should be preferred over the splits shipped with older benchmarks.

### Benchmark leakage and contamination

Many published numbers on older benchmarks are misleading because modern training sets overlap heavily with the test sets. A docking model trained on any pre-2021 PDB snapshot has effectively memorized large chunks of PDBbind and CASF. MoleculeNet's HIV and BACE sets contain near-duplicate pairs that survive scaffold splitting. DUD-E's actives and decoys are separable by crude physicochemical descriptors, so a model that looks like it discriminates binders may only be discriminating drug-like from non-drug-like chemistry (LIT-PCBA is a recommended replacement). PoseBusters mitigates leakage with a post-2021 set and PLINDER with similarity-aware splits — but many published comparisons do neither. When you read a new modeling paper, the first question is: *what was the split, and is the benchmark known to leak?*

### Activity cliffs and assay-merge noise

Aggregated bioactivity databases merge data from tens of thousands of distinct assays run across decades and labs. Even for the same compound against the same target, reported IC50 values can disagree by more than an order of magnitude — assay format, cell line, reference compound, substrate concentration, and plate-to-plate variation all contribute, and in the worst case the same molecule is "active" in one assay and "inactive" in another. Worse, the literature is full of **activity cliffs**, where a tiny change — a methyl group, a swapped heteroatom — shifts potency 10–100×. Models trained on naively merged data learn neither the real structure–activity signal nor a calibrated noise floor. Practical mitigations: filter to a single assay type per target before modeling (at a cost in dataset size), model assay identity as an explicit categorical feature, report per-assay as well as global performance, and validate on held-out *series* rather than held-out random samples.

### Commercial vs. free licensing

Several widely cited resources have moved to or reinforced commercial terms — some even since this appendix was first drafted. DrugBank's full database, KEGG for commercial use, and the Reaxys/SciFinder/Pistachio reaction databases all carry licensing constraints. If you are building something you intend to publish, distribute, or commercialize, audit the license terms of *every* resource in your pipeline early, not after the model works.

### Garbage in, garbage out

ML accelerates drug discovery, but suspect data yields a faulty model — *garbage in, garbage out*. Low data diversity narrows a model's **applicability domain**: the region of chemical space where its predictions remain trustworthy shrinks toward the well-explored structures it has actually seen. And even good data is only an approximation of reality.

Consider screening candidates for **drug-induced liver injury** (DILI), the leading cause of drug withdrawal from market [1]. Say you have a dataset of molecules labeled "Yes"/"No" for DILI association and you build a binary classifier. Send it to a pharmacovigilance team and the first thing they will tell you is that the labels are *conditional*: a drug might cause DILI only in patients with a certain genotype, or only alongside another drug, or only above a certain dose (even water is toxic at extreme doses). Severity, frequency across age and sex, and duration all vary. A binary classifier cannot represent that, and the further your data drifts from the full clinical context, the less a performance gain actually means for safety.

It gets subtler when you ask how such a dataset is made. In-vivo DILI measurement is expensive, low-throughput, and confounded, so practitioners often substitute a **proxy assay** — bile-salt export pump (BSEP) inhibition in hepatocytes, say. Proxies are useful, but the endpoint you care about is the *phenotype*. The further the data moves from in-vivo context, the further the model's utility moves from clinical relevance. These limitations are also opportunities — much of the second half of the book is about navigating them.

> **Note** — Even high-quality data can produce poor results if the context of its collection is ignored or misunderstood. Always investigate the experimental conditions under which your training data was generated.

## Where to look next

The resources catalogued here are a snapshot. New databases appear, old ones change license terms, scales grow by orders of magnitude, and curation practices evolve. Community review articles — *Nucleic Acids Research* publishes a database issue every January — are a good way to stay current. Whatever you use, cite the specific version or release (the ChEMBL release number, the UniProt release date, the Open Targets quarterly tag) and archive the snapshot you trained on. Models are only reproducible if the data behind them is.
